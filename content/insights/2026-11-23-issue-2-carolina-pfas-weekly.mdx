## ⚡ The Top Line: New Hanover County Turns Its $17 Million Chemours Settlement Share into a Public-Water Connection Program for PFAS-Impacted Well Owners

- **Defining Number:** **$17 million over 10 years · $5 million first phase · ~508 properties · up to $25,000 per connection beside existing lines — the first county allocation plan from the $590 million Chemours settlement**
- **Primary Sources (`NC-17`, `NC-17`):** [https://tossonanalytics.com/policy-tracker](https://tossonanalytics.com/policy-tracker) · [https://news.nhcgov.com/m/newsflash/Home/Detail/1207](https://news.nhcgov.com/m/newsflash/Home/Detail/1207)

On November 16, 2026, the New Hanover County Board of Commissioners unanimously approved a project ordinance creating a public-water connection program funded from the county's share of the September 2026 Chemours settlement — $17 million to the county over the next 10 years. The program will pay to connect residential and nonresidential properties with PFAS-impacted private wells to existing and planned Cape Fear Public Utility Authority (CFPUA) water lines; an initial three-year phase will review applications beginning in early 2027. It is the first county-level allocation plan to surface from the $590 million settlement among Chemours, North Carolina, and 11 counties and municipal water systems. One caution readers should keep: this $17 million settlement share is entirely separate from the $17 million state Emerging Contaminants in Surface Water Supply and Drinking Water grant awarded to CFPUA in March 2026 — the same figure, but different money, and the two should never be merged.

## 1. North Carolina Layer (Primary Coverage — 3 Verified Items)

### 1. Inside New Hanover's Connection Program: $5 Million First Phase, ~508 Properties, Up to $25,000 per Connection

- **Published / Action Date:** `2026-11-16`
- **Verified Source (`NC-17` — Wilmington StarNews (Daniel Sheehan, Nov 20, 2026) — program mechanics from the county’s Nov 16 presentation; tracker record NC-17):** [https://www.starnewsonline.com/story/news/environment/2026/11/20/new-hanover-to-invest-millions-for-water-lines-in-wilmington-nc/92727251007/](https://www.starnewsonline.com/story/news/environment/2026/11/20/new-hanover-to-invest-millions-for-water-lines-in-wilmington-nc/92727251007/) · [Secondary Source](https://tossonanalytics.com/policy-tracker)
- **NC Counties / Watersheds:** New Hanover County (CFPUA service area)
- **Compounds Tracked:** PFAS in private drinking-water wells (documented contamination required)
- **Screening Benchmark:** $17M / 10 years · $5M phase 1 · ~508 properties · $25,000 / $10,000 connection tiers

The mechanics, as detailed by the Wilmington StarNews (November 20, 2026) from the county's November 16 presentation: in the first three years the county will spend $5 million of its $17 million settlement share on CFPUA water connections for approximately 508 property owners. For properties adjacent to existing water lines, the county will fund up to $25,000 per connection — 47 impacted properties have been identified in that category. A further 461 properties would be reached through 15 CFPUA water-main extension projects in the areas of the county with the highest PFAS concentrations, funded at $10,000 per connection. The program is anticipated to begin in early 2027. The county's own meeting recap confirms the unanimous project-ordinance vote, the $17 million over 10 years, the initial three-year program, and applications beginning in early 2027.

> **Why It Matters:** This is settlement money becoming infrastructure on a published timetable — the first concrete answer to what the $590 million settlement means at the household level. It also sets the template the other 10 local governments in the settlement will be measured against as their own allocation plans surface.

### 2. The Adjacency Line: Who New Hanover's Program Reaches — and Who Still Waits on Filtration

- **Published / Action Date:** `2026-11-20`
- **Verified Source (`NC-17` — New Hanover County — Commissioner Meeting Recap, Nov 16, 2026 (program approval & eligibility framework; detail via StarNews)):** [https://news.nhcgov.com/m/newsflash/Home/Detail/1207](https://news.nhcgov.com/m/newsflash/Home/Detail/1207) · [Secondary Source](https://www.starnewsonline.com/story/news/environment/2026/11/20/new-hanover-to-invest-millions-for-water-lines-in-wilmington-nc/92727251007/)
- **NC Counties / Watersheds:** New Hanover County
- **Compounds Tracked:** PFAS (documented well contamination; no concentration threshold)
- **Screening Benchmark:** Eligibility: adjacency + documented PFAS + willingness to pay CFPUA usage ($5.95 / 1,000 gal through June 2027)

Eligibility for the New Hanover connection program turns on three tests, per the county's presentation reported by the StarNews: the property must sit directly adjacent to an existing or planned CFPUA water main; the owner must show documented PFAS contamination in a private drinking-water well (test results are required, in part to avoid duplicating connections the Chemours Consent Order already requires); and the owner must be willing to take on an ongoing CFPUA water bill after connection — the current usage rate is $5.95 per 1,000 gallons through June 2027. Notably, there are no PFAS-concentration threshold and no income restrictions, and CFPUA's planned main extensions are aimed at the county's most heavily impacted areas, including low-to-moderate income communities. Properties that fail the adjacency test are not in this program at all: their routes remain Consent Order filtration, the state Alternate Water Program, or self-funded treatment.

> **Why It Matters:** Adjacency is a hard geographic line through a contamination footprint that does not follow water-main maps. The program will fully solve drinking water for ~508 properties while leaving equally contaminated wells a street away on filtration maintenance — which is why the maintenance question in the next item matters beyond Cumberland County.

### 3. The Maintenance Gap DEQ's Deficiency Letter Didn't Cover: GAC Filtration Upkeep Under Consent Order Paragraph 24

- **Published / Action Date:** `2026-11-17`
- **Verified Source (`NC-02` — NC DEQ — GenX Investigation & Consent Order Portal (Drinking Water Compliance Plan, Paragraph 24)):** [https://edocs.deq.nc.gov:443/WasteManagement/DocView.aspx?id=1636613&dbid=0&repo=WasteManagement&cr=1](https://edocs.deq.nc.gov:443/WasteManagement/DocView.aspx?id=1636613&dbid=0&repo=WasteManagement&cr=1) · [Secondary Source](https://devieredispatch.substack.com/p/holding-chemours-to-its-word)
- **NC Counties / Watersheds:** Cumberland County (Gray's Creek and the Fayetteville Works well footprint)
- **Docket / Rule:** 2019 Consent Order Paragraph 24 — Drinking Water Compliance Plan (GAC operations, maintenance & monitoring)
- **Compounds Tracked:** GenX & Attachment C PFAS (whole-house GAC filtration)

DEQ's November 9 deficiency direction (Issue #1) addresses assessment and cleanup planning — but not the upkeep of the whole-house granular activated carbon (GAC) systems already installed in affected homes. Under the Drinking Water Compliance Plan required by Paragraph 24 of the 2019 Consent Order, Chemours must maintain those systems: iron filters are to be serviced on a regular cycle, and carbon tanks replaced at 100,000 gallons or one year, whichever comes first. Writing this month, Cumberland County Commissioner deViere argues that maintenance is not being performed to that standard and that DEQ's cleanup-planning direction leaves the gap untouched. Chemours' stated position, given earlier this year, is that it installs and maintains the GAC systems in accordance with the protocols agreed with DEQ — including quarterly monitoring and sampling, with change-outs when sampling results meet established thresholds — and that iron and bacteria conditions are private-well conditions outside the PFAS program; residents with service concerns are directed to the Chemours Help Line, (910) 678-1101.

> **Why It Matters:** For households whose permanent remedy is a filter rather than a water line, maintenance is the remedy. A GAC system past its carbon-replacement interval is not a partial protection — it is an expired one, and the dispute over who checks the calendar is now the live front of Consent Order enforcement in Cumberland County.


## 🚰 North Carolina Private Wells Corner: A Fourth Door Opens in New Hanover County — Settlement-Funded Connections Join the Three Standing Pathways

- **Verified NC Well-User Population Basis:** 2.4 million (NC DHHS OEEB conservative baseline) to 3.3 million / 35% of state population (USGS 2010 baseline, Maupin et al. 2014 via MacDonald Gibson et al. 2017, PMC5744693); ~2.8 million via CWFNC 2005 public-supply subtraction estimate.

UCMR 5 did not sample a single private well, leaving North Carolina's unregulated domestic well population — conservatively estimated at 2.4 million residents by the NC Department of Health and Human Services (NC DHHS) and at approximately 3.3 million residents (35% of the state, the second-highest total in the nation after Pennsylvania) in USGS water-use data analyzed by MacDonald Gibson et al. (Environ. Health Perspect., 2017) — entirely outside federal tap-water surveillance. As Clean Water for North Carolina Senior Advisor Hope Taylor observed in a November 2026 exchange with Tosson Analytics, subtracting reported public-water-supply customers from total state population already yielded ~2.8 million residents in 2005 before two decades of rural well permitting. For North Carolina well owners seeking testing and treatment today, four verified pathways now exist: (1) NEW — Settlement-Funded Public-Water Connections in New Hanover County: Approved November 16, 2026 and opening to applications in early 2027, the county's program pays up to $25,000 per connection for properties adjacent to existing CFPUA lines and $10,000 per connection through 15 planned main-extension projects (~508 properties in the $5M first phase), for owners with documented PFAS in their wells; there is no PFAS-concentration threshold and no income restriction, but connected owners take on the CFPUA usage bill ($5.95 per 1,000 gallons through June 2027), and properties not adjacent to existing or planned mains do not qualify; (2) Free Consent-Order Sampling across 10 Counties: Under the Chemours Consent Order enforced by NC DEQ, Parsons Environment and Infrastructure — reachable at (910) 678-1100 or (910) 678-1101 — is the sole authorized sampler. DEQ reported on November 9, 2026 that approximately 27,500 household wells have been sampled under the Consent Order and approximately 10,500 families offered filtration to date, following NC DEQ's March 10, 2025 expansion (+150,000 residences across Cumberland, Bladen, Robeson, Sampson, and parts of Harnett and Hoke) and September 11, 2025 Lower Cape Fear expansion (+14,000 residences across New Hanover, Brunswick, Columbus, and Pender); wells screening at ≥10 ppt GenX or any listed Attachment C PFAS (or ≥70 ppt combined) qualify for replacement water, RO/GAC filtration, or municipal connection at Chemours' expense; (3) Statewide Free RO or Up to $3,500 Reimbursement: Where no responsible party is identified and private-well results exceed a state or federal standard, NC DEQ's PFAS Alternate Water Program (backed by the Bernard Allen Memorial Emergency Drinking Water Fund, funded with an additional $10.2M in the enacted July 2026 state budget) provides a free under-sink RO system or up to $3,500 in first-come, first-served reimbursement for qualifying treatment installed after November 2019; and (4) Certified Private Lab Panels: Outside free programs, county health departments test bacteria and nitrates ($0–$60) but do not run PFAS; homeowners must order an accredited EPA Method 533 (25 compounds) or Method 537.1 (18 compounds) panel directly from a certified lab (starting around $380 at NC private labs and $300–$600 nationally) and screen their lab report against EPA's MCL benchmarks (4.0 ppt PFOA/PFOS, 10.0 ppt GenX/PFNA/PFHxS).

## 📊 Tosson Data Corner: The Exemption Math: How 131,411 PFAS Reporters Become 255

- **Tracker Snapshot:** `131,411 → 255 entities (−99.8%) · 67,548 → 1,461 reports (−97.8%) · −3.4 million reporting hours (EPA projections in FR 2026-23125, Nov 13, 2026)`
- **Verified Sources (`US-01`, `NC-17`):** [https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2026-11-13/pdf/2026-23125.pdf](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2026-11-13/pdf/2026-23125.pdf) · [https://tossonanalytics.com/policy-tracker](https://tossonanalytics.com/policy-tracker)

EPA's November 13, 2026 information-collection notice puts exact numbers on what the signaled TSCA Section 8(a)(7) exemptions would do to the federal PFAS census. Without the exemptions, the 2011–2022 lookback reaches an estimated 131,411 entities filing 67,548 reports. With the six exemptions incorporated — 0.1% de minimis, imported articles, byproducts, impurities, R&D chemicals, and non-isolated intermediates — EPA projects 255 entities and 1,461 reports: a 99.8% reduction in reporters and a 97.8% reduction in reports, saving an estimated 3.4 million burden hours. The single largest removal is imported articles: finished goods are how most PFAS enters US commerce, and article importers were the reporting rule's widest net. Set this beside the same week's North Carolina news — a county spending settlement money to find and connect contaminated wells one property at a time — and the two stories describe the same national posture from opposite ends: remediation proceeding household by household, while the upstream inventory of what was made and imported is narrowed by design. The figures are EPA's projections for a rule that is not yet final; comments on the collection notice are open through December 14, 2026, and the amendments have not yet reached OMB.

## 2. United States Layer (Federal & Comparative Signal — 2 Verified Items)

### 1. EPA Signals It Will Finalize the TSCA PFAS Reporting Exemptions — Cutting the 2011–2022 Inventory from ~131,000 Reporters to 255

- **Published / Action Date:** `2026-11-13`
- **Verified Source (`US-01` — Office of the Federal Register (NARA / GPO) — ICR Notice 2026-23125 (91 FR 73228)):** [https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2026-11-13/pdf/2026-23125.pdf](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2026-11-13/pdf/2026-23125.pdf) · [Secondary Source](https://www.verdantlaw.com/epa-indicates-it-will-finalize-proposed-pfas-reporting-exemptions/)
- **Docket / Rule:** EPA-HQ-OPPT-2020-0549 · FR 2026-23125 · EPA ICR 2682.04 / OMB 2070-0217
- **Compounds Tracked:** All PFAS (2011–2022 lookback)
- **Screening Benchmark:** Projected with exemptions: 255 entities / 1,461 reports (vs 131,411 / 67,548 without)

In an information-collection notice published November 13, 2026 (FR 2026-23125), EPA states that it intends to incorporate into the TSCA Section 8(a)(7) PFAS reporting rule the exemptions it proposed in November 2025: a 0.1% de minimis exemption, and exemptions for imported articles, byproducts, impurities, research and development chemicals, and non-isolated intermediates. EPA projects the exemptions would cut affected entities from 131,411 to 255 and required reports from 67,548 to 1,461, a reduction of about 3.4 million reporting hours. Two cautions belong in the same paragraph: the exemptions are not final — the rule amendments have not yet been received by OMB — and comments on the collection notice itself run through December 14, 2026. If finalized, the current January 31, 2027 reporting start would be replaced by a three-month window beginning 60 days after the final rule's effective date.

> **Why It Matters:** Section 8(a)(7) was designed as the federal government's one-time census of PFAS in commerce — who made or imported what, from 2011 to 2022. Exempting imported articles alone removes the largest single category of PFAS entering the country. Whatever the final rule's merits on burden, the census EPA ends up holding will describe a much smaller universe than the one Congress ordered counted.

## 3. Global Layer (International Signal — 1 Verified Item)

### 1. ECHA's Socio-Economic Committee Adopts Its Final Opinion Backing the EU-Wide PFAS Restriction — the Clock Moves to the European Commission

- **Published / Action Date:** `2026-11-19`
- **Verified Source (`GL-01` — European Chemicals Agency (ECHA) — SEAC final-opinion announcement (Nov 19, 2026), as reported by European Coatings):** [https://www.european-coatings.com/news/legislation/echa-backs-eu-wide-restrictions-on-pfas-and-chromium-vi/](https://www.european-coatings.com/news/legislation/echa-backs-eu-wide-restrictions-on-pfas-and-chromium-vi/) · [Secondary Source](https://echa.europa.eu/hot-topics/perfluoroalkyl-chemicals-pfas)

- **Docket / Rule:** SEAC final opinion adopted Nov 19, 2026 · RAC final opinion Mar 2026 · Commission proposal expected early 2027
- **Compounds Tracked:** Universal PFAS class (>10,000 substances)

On November 19, 2026, the European Chemicals Agency (ECHA) announced that its Committee for Socio-Economic Analysis (SEAC) has adopted its final opinion on the five-nation universal REACH restriction proposal covering more than 10,000 PFAS. SEAC concluded that EU-wide action is needed to address PFAS risks, prevent market distortions, and maintain a level playing field, and it supports a ban with use-specific derogations, with transition periods and time-limited derogations for sectors still developing alternatives. SEAC joins the Risk Assessment Committee (final opinion March 2026): both committees found existing regulatory measures insufficient to control PFAS emissions and risks. The opinions go to the European Commission in the coming weeks; a Commission restriction proposal is expected in early 2027, for discussion with member states in the REACH Committee. This is the adoption Issue #1 flagged as due by year-end — it landed on November 19.

> **Why It Matters:** The scientific-committee phase of the largest chemical restriction ever proposed is now complete. For North Carolina exporters — textiles, electronics, fluoropolymer supply chains — the question is no longer whether the EU restricts PFAS as a class, but which derogations the Commission writes and how long they run.


## 🔬 Research Radar: New Peer-Reviewed Literature

### Paper 1: Censoring-aware models quantify systematic underestimation of PFAS occurrence in US drinking-water monitoring

- **Authors:** Gehendra Kharel, Andrei P. Kirilenko, Omar R. Harvey, and Chris B. Zou
- **Journal & Year:** *Cell Reports Sustainability*, 2026 (Vol. 3, 100893; published Oct 23, 2026)
- **Identifiers:** DOI `10.1016/j.crsus.2026.100893` · Source ID `US-07`
- **Full-URL Links:** [https://www.cell.com/cell-reports-sustainability/fulltext/S2949-7906(26)00262-4](https://www.cell.com/cell-reports-sustainability/fulltext/S2949-7906(26)00262-4) · [https://doi.org/10.1016/j.crsus.2026.100893](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.crsus.2026.100893)

Researchers at Texas Christian University, the University of Florida, and Oklahoma State University reanalyzed EPA's UCMR 5 dataset (January 2026 release: ~1.86 million PFAS records; 9,991 community water systems in the primary analysis), of which about 98% of results sit below laboratory minimum reporting levels (MRLs). Building a censoring-aware multiple-imputation framework and benchmarking it against censored maximum-likelihood and other estimators, they find that the common practice of treating below-MRL results as zero captures only 44% of the model-estimated population-weighted occurrence for 25 PFAS analytes, and 50% for the six PFAS in the 2024 drinking-water regulation framework (for those six, a zero-substitution average of ~2.6 ng/L versus a modeled ~5.2 ng/L). PFOA and PFOS account for 27–31% of the censoring gap; gaps persist across model families, water sources, and system sizes.

> **Scope & Claim Boundary (§1.3):** These are model-based estimates of concentrations below reporting limits, not new measurements; the authors state the analysis does not determine regulatory compliance or health risk, and near-MCL screening changes were modest. The magnitude of the gap varies by statistical model family, though zero substitution sat below every accepted censored-likelihood estimate tested.

### Paper 2: Historical Blood Serum Samples from Wilmington, North Carolina: The Importance of Ultrashort-Chain Per- and Polyfluoroalkyl Substances

- **Authors:** Lan Cheng, Sarah Teagle, Jeffrey R. Enders, Rebecca A. Weed, Hazel B. Nichols, Detlef R. U. Knappe, and Jane A. Hoppin
- **Journal & Year:** *Environmental Science & Technology*, 2025 (Vol. 59, Issue 43; published Oct 27, 2025)
- **Identifiers:** DOI `10.1021/acs.est.5c08146` · PMID `41143432` · Source ID `US-07`
- **Full-URL Links:** [https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41143432/](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41143432/) · [https://doi.org/10.1021/acs.est.5c08146](https://doi.org/10.1021/acs.est.5c08146)

NC State and UNC-Chapel Hill researchers quantified 56 PFAS — including ultrashort-chain compounds earlier methods could not reliably detect in blood — in 119 archived adult serum samples collected from Wilmington-area residents in 2010–2016, before the contamination was public, plus 2017 drinking-water samples. Twenty PFAS appeared in more than half the serum samples. The ultrashort-chain compounds perfluoromethoxyacetic acid (PFMOAA) and trifluoroacetic acid (TFA) dominated: median serum concentrations of 42 and 17 ng/mL respectively, and together 95% of the quantifiable PFAS in the 2017 drinking water (TFA ~108,000 ng/L; PFMOAA ~38,000 ng/L in a May 2017 sample), with Fayetteville Works identified as the dominant source of both in the lower Cape Fear River. GenX — the compound that drove public concern — was detected in only about 20% of samples, consistent with its short biological half-life.

> **Scope & Claim Boundary (§1.3):** Archived, anonymized samples from a cancer-survivorship cohort biobank: a historical exposure timestamp (2010–2016), not a measure of current exposure — the authors note present-day levels of these compounds have likely decreased substantially. Ultrashort-chain PFAS toxicology remains thin, and TFA has additional sources (including fluorinated refrigerants) beyond the facility.
